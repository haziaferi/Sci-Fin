The scoring could not separate these: 0.785 against 0.776, and across all 1771 weightings the unsplit policy wins 43.3% against the split policy's 31.1% — a difference inside the noise of how the weights are chosen. Every other question is settled. This one comes down to what you can see at once, so here it is drawn rather than scored.

Both columns below carry identical content — the same five envelope and category items — and the same fixed 88px rail. The only difference is the rule applied to the content area. Both panes scroll, as every measured app does — nothing is unreachable in either option. The number that separates them is therefore not what is hidden but how far you must scroll to reach the last category, measured below.

The question, as narrowly as it can be put — and it is narrower than the first draft of this sheet claimed. That draft drew a red “below the fold” line, which implied content was lost. It is not: both options scroll, like every app measured. Measured properly, B costs 125px of scrolling — about two cards — and that figure is constant: widening the window from 1084 to 1800 adds 716px of empty space and removes none of it. A removes the scroll entirely above the floor, at the price of a rule that applies to exactly one destination.

So the honest trade is 125px of permanent scroll against one extra layout rule, and that is a much smaller gap than “below the fold” made it look. The thing no measurement can weigh is whether seeing envelopes and categories together is what Budget is for. If the side-by-side view is the point of the screen, A earns its exception. If Budget is a list you scroll like any other, two cards of scrolling is not worth a third mechanism, and B is right.
