The desktop, six ways

§4.8 was reopened on 2026-09-23: “the desktop app will not be a copy of the phone app.” That section had decided the opposite — adaptive reflow over a separate desktop shell — and it decided it on register grounds rather than on cost, so its argument is not withdrawn. It is the case each option below has to answer:

“What authoring one actually buys is density: use the width to show more at once — Envelopes and Categories side by side rather than behind §4.3’s segmented control, Activity as a dashboard grid, every figure visible simultaneously. That payoff is one this app is designed not to want. §4.2 assigns Budget the habit-loop pattern and warns in its own words that Categories is ‘the screen most tempting to default to status-board’ … A desktop IA that surfaces every figure at once converts the app’s most-visited screen into exactly the scoreboard §2.2 and §2.4 exist to prevent.”

So the question is not “should the desktop use the width”. It is what the width buys, if it may not buy simultaneity of figures. There are four honest answers to that, and each is an option below: nothing (B), navigation (A), context (C), size (D). Two more options challenge the premise rather than answer it: E spends the width on a second view, and F spends the left edge on data instead of icons.

What the survey changed added 2026-09-23, after the fact

Seven open-source desktop finance apps were surveyed — six of them fully weighted, Maybe discounted because it is no longer maintained — and every option here was scored against them. The full record is design/desktop-prior-art-2026-09-23.md; three findings bear directly on the frames below, and the first one weakens the blockquote above.

  • Width and verdict-colour are independent choices. §4.8’s sentence treats them as one thing — show every figure at once and you get the scoreboard. KMyMoney is the counterexample: the widest, most figure-dense budget layout in the survey (budget list, full category tree, and an assignments panel, all at once) with no over/under colour coding at all. Actual Budget is the other side of the pair — it applies red to overspent envelopes and green to “every euro assigned”, and measuring its computed colours shows it colours verdicts, not negative numbers: -770.24 and -170.12 render in ordinary text. So Sci-Fin can take the width without taking the scoreboard, provided it keeps §2.1 — which it already mandates as a prohibition. Weakened later the same day: a second app was briefly cited as confirming this and does not — see the correction under The survey’s “data sidebar” count was wrong. This now rests on KMyMoney alone, which is documentation evidence, so treat it as an open question rather than a finding.
  • Nobody uses an icon rail. Zero of seven. Five use a data sidebar, one tabs, one dockable panels. Actual’s sidebar measures 240px at this exact window size — 2.7× Sci-Fin’s 88px rail — because it carries every account and its balance rather than five icons. That is what F below draws.
  • Side-by-side exists in the wild, but only once, and not in E’s form. Actual pairs it with a data sidebar and inline editors; E pairs it with a rail and dialogs. The field does not support E’s particular combination — which is most of why the scoring below goes the way it does.

Why categories work

People do not treat all money the same. A pound set aside for rent is not felt as the same pound as one left loose, even though nothing about it differs — which is the finding envelope budgeting has always quietly relied on.

That is why this screen splits one figure into named portions instead of showing you one balance and a target to hit. The portions are the mechanism, not decoration.

Nothing here turns red when a portion runs low. A category over its plan is information, not a verdict.

The scale ceiling, measured under the rule that text does not scale

Text is OS-only — the window may not override it — and radii and strokes came off the screen axis in the same decision. Frame D already obeys this: it scales u 8→13px and pad 14→23px while all seven type tokens stay at their root values.

The honest conclusion, which no option escapes: spacing alone cannot fill a maximised window. Even at a frankly implausible u of 24px — triple the phone’s gutters — a 2560×1440 window is still only 64% full. Because text may not grow, a card’s height is dominated by a term the window is not allowed to touch, so padding it out hits diminishing returns long before the screen is full. Two consequences. First, the ceiling is a minor decision, not the major one — over the whole plausible range 1.0×–2.0× the fill at 1920 moves from 46.6% to 66.1%, and every value in it leaves the window substantially empty. Second, fullscreen fill should not be what picks between these options, because none of them solve it; what it does do is disqualify the option that is worst at the size the app actually opens at. That is still C, at 42.5%.

Everything above measures ink — how much of the window is covered. That is the wrong proxy, and the objection that exposed it is simple: a card at twice the spacing covers twice the pixels and tells you exactly the same thing. Decision: E chosen 2026-09-23, on information efficiency

I withdraw “E on Budget, C’s rule elsewhere”. It was the recommendation in the previous version of this sheet and the survey does not support it. Scored against the field it comes 56th of 180 feasible structures, below every option drawn here, and it beats plain C under 0% of 4,000 random reweightings of the six fitness components. That is not a close call I can argue around by re-weighting.

The argument I made for it still stands as an observation — Budget really is the only tab in §4.2’s table with two co-equal views of one thing, and the other four really are list-pushes-to-detail. What I got wrong was the price. I costed it as “editors must push or use a dialog”. The real cost is that E+C forks three things from the phone build at once — side-by-side Budget, reserved right pane, dialog editors — where C forks one. That penalty (−0.044 weighted) is larger than the figure-density penalty (−0.056 against a much smaller base) that §4.8’s objection was actually about. And the prior-art term did not rescue it: it scored E+C below C, because the one app that ships side-by-side pairs it with a data sidebar and inline editors, not a rail and dialogs.

And I withdraw C as well. It was the pick for about as long as it took to measure it. C won the first fitness on a term I had assumed rather than measured — a reserved pane scored as a used pane. It is not one: at 2560×1440 it is 85.3% empty, the worst of the six at every size tested. Reserving half the window for content that has not been written yet is the weakest thing on this sheet, not the strongest.

And D is withdrawn in turn. I recommended it one revision ago on the strength of ink fill. On the criterion that matters it is the worst option on the sheet: four money figures and one bar, against E’s eighteen and seven. Filling a window with larger cards and larger bars is not using the width, it is padding it.

The decision is E — Envelopes and Categories together — taken on information efficiency. It shows the most of any option, 25 atoms against D’s 5, in less ink than D uses, and it is the only option where the extra width carries extra meaning rather than extra spacing. It is also the only one whose advantage grows rather than evaporates as the window grows, because two columns of real content scale with the space where one column of enlarged furniture does not.

What is being accepted, knowingly. §4.8 named this exact arrangement as its argument against a desktop shell, and the figures_density term scores E at 0.72 where every single-view option scores 1.00. That cost is real and it is being taken deliberately, on two grounds the survey supplies. First, KMyMoney proves density and verdict are separable — the widest, most figure-dense budget layout surveyed carries no over/under colour coding at all. Second, Actual shows what the failure actually looks like when it happens: red on overspent envelopes, green on “every euro assigned” — colour attached to a verdict. §2.1’s prohibition is therefore not a nicety under E, it is the single load-bearing constraint. Density without valence is a dense screen; density with valence is the scoreboard §2.2 and §2.4 exist to prevent.

The Learn hook survives — as a card, not a pane. C’s actual good idea was that the Budget screen should be able to say why it looks like this. Nothing about that required half the window; it required a card at the foot of the column. Kept that way it costs one component, needs no per-screen reservation rule, and stops blocking on §4.5’s four unwritten bodies — the dependency that made C the slowest option to build disappears with the pane.

What E settles, and what it does not. E is an answer to Budget. It is the only tab in §4.2’s table with two co-equal views of one thing, so the pairing does not generalise — and the four remaining tabs are now unanswered, because C was carrying them in the withdrawn synthesis and C is gone. Activity, Learn, Reports and Settings are list-pushes-to-detail; the honest default for them is A’s reflow, which costs nothing because it already exists, with Reports staying single-pane and wide as §4.8 already excepted. That should be confirmed rather than assumed.

Three things stay open under E. (1) Where editors go — now largely answered. KMyMoney, installed and walked on 2026-09-23, puts its ledger editor in a persistent bottom strip beneath the list, tabbed by entry kind. That is a fourth option alongside push, dialog and right pane, and it is the one that fits E: E consumes both columns but leaves the vertical axis free, so an editor can sit along the bottom without disturbing either view. §4.8 rule 3 (sheets become dialogs) should lapse in favour of it. (2) The rail. Zero of seven surveyed apps use an icon rail and five use a 240px data sidebar — and that question is orthogonal to E, not settled by it. E with F’s sidebar is a live combination that would raise the information count further still; it is worth drawing before the rail is taken for granted. (3) The scale ceiling, which the measurements show is a small decision: across 1.0×–2.0× a 1920 window moves only from 46.6% to 66.1% full, because text may not scale.

The survey’s “data sidebar” count was wrong

§2 of the survey recorded “five of seven use a data sidebar”. That collapsed two different shells. Fava, measured at 1100×760, uses a 160px text-nav sidebar — sixteen named destinations, no icons, no figures — as do Money Manager Ex, Firefly III and Maybe. Only Actual puts live balances there. So F as drawn copies Actual specifically, not the field; the field’s real consensus is narrower, and it is only “do not use an icon rail”, which still stands at 0 of 9.

Correction — the sentence that stood here was wrong. It claimed Fava was a second counterexample to §4.8, showing “density without verdict”. The measurement behind it queried table figures and silently excluded the chart, which is SVG. Re-measured:

• Figures carry no valence — negatives render rgb(204,204,204) like everything else. Sign gets no colour. That part holds.
• The chart carries 47 distinct fills across the whole hue circle (0–353°), 7 in the red band and 5 in the green. Category identifiers rather than a good/bad encoding — but 47 colours breaches §2.5’s one-accent-per-theme regardless.
• The verdict is in the language, not the colour. The screen is titled Income Statement and leads with Net Profit −103,335.53, beside Balance Sheet and Trial Balance. That is performance accounting — a screen whose job is to tell you whether you did well. §2.2/§2.4 rule out that framing, and neutral colour does not rescue it.

Fava is therefore not a register model for this app. It supports one narrow claim — sign need not be coloured — and on register it is closer to what §4.8 feared than to a rebuttal of it.

And that weakens the claim E was accepted on. “Width and verdict are independent” now rests on KMyMoney alone, which is documentation evidence — its handbook not describing colour coding is not the same as observing it has none. Not disproven: Actual’s red/green is still demonstrably a YNAB-inherited method choice rather than a consequence of width. But not established either. It should be treated as an open question rather than a finding, and it was doing load-bearing work in the decision to accept E’s density.

Sections above described KMyMoney from its documentation and its Budgets view alone. It was then installed from download.kde.org, driven at 1100 × 760, walked across every destination, and removed. The single-screen reading was wrong.

Seven destinations, at least six layouts, and no rule connecting them. Lists, editors and the use of width are re-decided on every screen. From Budget alone it looks like a considered three-pane design; walked in full, that view is one of seven ad-hoc answers. This is what an app looks like when the layout rule is never written down once — and it is an argument for §4.8 stating one explicitly, which is what C’s reservation rule was reaching for even though C’s own proportions were wrong.

The Budget proportions are wrong in an instructive way. The budget list — one or two rows for almost every user — takes 39% of the window, while the whole working area gets 531px and must split three ways vertically. Width allocated by category of content rather than by quantity: the same error as scoring a reserved pane at a flat 1.00 without measuring its fill.

Two ideas survive the walk. The bottom editor strip (see the open questions below, which it answers). And the Budgets view’s Periodo radio group — Mensile / Annuale / Individuale — reshaping the editor beneath it, so a category’s budget is one monthly figure, one yearly figure, or twelve individual months in the same panel. The second is a feature idea, not a layout, and does not bear on A–F.

Actual Budget walked, every screen 2026-09-23 — the same walk as KMyMoney

Nine screens, driven at 1100 × 760 in the public demo and measured from the DOM. Against KMyMoney's seven screens and six improvised layouts, this is the clearest structural contrast in the survey.

The shell never changes — 240 / 860 on all nine. What changes is the inner measure, and it changes by kind of content: tables take 820–849, the budget table takes 636, and Settings takes 530 centred — a reading measure, not a table measure. The sidebar is fixed rather than proportional: when the viewport dropped to 815px mid-walk it stayed at exactly 240 and the main pane absorbed the whole loss.

That is a third formulation neither C nor E proposed. Not reserve a pane, not pair two views — fix the shell and vary the measure. Fava independently lands in the same family (160px text-nav sidebar, one main pane), which makes it the modal answer among the apps that are actually pleasant to use. And Actual's Settings measure of 530 centred is almost exactly candidate B's answer, applied to one screen rather than to the whole app — B scored mid-table as a whole-app strategy, but as a per-content-type rule it is what the best-regarded app in the survey ships.

Editing is inline, in the cell. Clicking a category turns the cell itself into a combobox with a contextual dropdown of categories and their balances. There are now four observed answers to “where do editors go”: inline in the cell (Actual), a persistent bottom strip (KMyMoney's ledger), a modal dialog (most of KMyMoney, Money Manager Ex), and push to a detail screen (Sci-Fin's phone build).

Stated straight, without reopening the decision: E's two columns are a departure from what both modern apps do — under this rule Budget would keep §4.3's segmented control and spend the width on a wider measure, which is closer to A or B. E stands; it is your call and the information-efficiency argument for it is unaffected. But two things are adoptable under E regardless: the measure rule (tables wide, prose and settings ~530 centred) stated once in §4.8, which is the part of C worth keeping after C was withdrawn; and inline editing, which strengthens the case for letting §4.8 rule 3 lapse.

GnuCash and Money Manager EX, installed and walked 2026-09-23 — completing the set

Both installed from their official GitHub releases, driven at 1100 × 760, walked and removed. Both are current releases, unlike KMyMoney's 2022 build.

GnuCash 5.16 — one shell, per-tab toolbars

No sidebar anywhere: a tabbed notebook with one full-width pane per tab, and tabs accumulate (accounts → a register → each report). What changes per screen is the toolbar, not the layout. Accounts gets Nuovo/Modifica/Elimina; a register gets Duplica/Inserisci/Vuota/Trasferimento/Riconcilia; a report gets Indietro / Avanti / Ricarica / Stop / Esporta / Stampa — browser navigation. Editing is inline in the row, as in Actual. Accounts and registers carry a summary bar at the foot; report tabs drop it.

GnuCash's report tab is the clearest emptiness failure in the whole survey. The report renders as a narrow left-aligned document occupying roughly 470 of 1084px, with no centring and no second pane — worse than anything measured in Actual, and a direct caution for any option that renders prose or a chart into a wide pane without setting a measure.

Money Manager EX 1.9.4 — one shell, one reused list template

Navigatore panel ~197 | workspace ~891. The dashboard is a two-column grid (accounts table | chart plus totals) under a summary strip. Tutte le Transazioni and Conti Bancari are the same template twice — identical filter strip, columns and bottom action row. Editing is modal dialogs, and report configuration is a large modal too. Two oddities worth recording: the chart legend uses green for income and red for expenses, and the Budget screen's empty state is the in-app manual, rendered in place with back/forward — the screen with nothing to show teaches you what it is for.

Whichever of A–F is built, that sentence is what keeps it from drifting into KMyMoney's condition.
